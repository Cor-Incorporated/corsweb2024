## Turns Out, I Was the Alien

In kindergarten, I couldn't join in when everyone played together. Sent outside, I would just pedal a tricycle round and round the yard.

At my elementary school entrance ceremony, a butterfly fluttered across my view, and I simply chased it right out of the gymnasium.

Once, wanting to see if you could really start a fire with a magnifying glass, I spent an entire lunch break focusing sunlight on a pile of fallen leaves. It ended up as a small fire.

To me, all of it made complete sense. There was something I wanted to do, and I just took the most direct path to it. But looking back, to the people around me, I must have seemed like a child whose actions were entirely unpredictable.

By the fourth grade, nobody invited me out to play anymore.

So, I began devouring books on behavioral psychology at the library. Gaze, gestures, pauses. Ways to read what people felt from the outside, without relying on their spoken words. By the sixth grade, I was even trusted enough to serve as class president.

Yet, I was constantly exhausted. Reading people’s expressions, choosing words that wouldn't disrupt the mood. Talking like that meant the conversation would end without what I truly thought ever getting across.

Wasn't there a more natural way to convey feelings? That thought stayed with me from childhood onward.

## I Heard the Audience Catch Its Breath

I had been taking piano lessons since I was five, influenced by my mother, who was a middle school music teacher. The piano was fun. But on stage, I was always alone.

In the sixth grade, I heard a trumpet fanfare at a local junior orchestra concert.

*This is it*, I thought. Speaking through sound with different people, creating a single piece of music together. Feelings words alone could never fully convey would surely reach others this way. As soon as I got home, I begged my grandparents to buy me a trumpet.

However, the trumpet wouldn't sound. Even after four months in my middle school brass band, I couldn't produce a proper note. The people around me laughed.

I broke down the sheet music for our assigned piece into two-measure segments. If I could play it five times in a row without a single mistake, I moved on to the next two measures. Once I reached the end, I re-segmented the piece into four-measure chunks and started over from the beginning. That was all I did throughout summer vacation.

The winter of my second year in high school. At a Christmas concert in a hall packed with 2,000 people, I played a solo.

Under the spotlight, I couldn't see anything in the audience. The moment I released the first note, I heard the audience catch its breath.

No words were needed. Nor was there any need to read anyone's face.

I wanted to feel that sense of unity once more, so I chose the path of music.

## That Moment Never Came Again

The first winter after entering the trumpet department of an art university, a trivial misunderstanding with a professor led to me being treated as if I were invisible within the department. That lasted for months, and the rumors that spread back then followed me until the day I quit music.

Once again, I went back to living while constantly reading people’s expressions. Even while performing, my mind was busy calculating: "What are they thinking of me right now?" Smiling outwardly, collapsing the moment I got home. Days when I couldn't get out of bed dragged on, and I began seeing a psychiatrist.

At 28, I was diagnosed with ASD (Autism Spectrum Disorder).

Honestly, it came as a relief. The difference between myself and others that I had felt for so long finally had a name.

After graduating, I worked at a musical instrument manufacturer for about four years, promoting instruments. While continuing to take on performance gigs, I also considered studying abroad and taught at schools.

Still, that feeling from the winter of my second year in high school never returned.

## In 2020, Everything Came to a Halt

In April 2020, under the state of emergency, every single performance was canceled. My income as a freelance performer dropped to zero.

Could work really vanish so easily? Clinging to music like this would leave me unable to survive.

I joined Recruit and became a corporate sales representative for job advertisements. Having known only the music world, I wanted to throw myself into the midst of diverse people. Talking to a different business owner every day was refreshing.

Yet here too, words failed to reach them. The moment I stated my company name, the call would be disconnected. Even if I managed to meet someone, I would be told, "I'm not the decision-maker, so just leave your materials." And when I finally met a decision-maker, I could only propose solutions within the confines of the company's designated tools.

## Code Never Betrayed Me

Tired of building call lists, I learned web scraping with Python. All I wanted was to automate tedious work. But before I knew it, I was researching what Python could do every single day. That was when I came across the GPT-3 paper.

I proposed an AI concierge system through an internal new-business program. The result was rejection in the first round. I was told, "It's just pie in the sky."

About six months later, ChatGPT came out.

I went back to study programming at G's ACADEMY FUKUOKA.

With code, what you envision can be turned directly into reality. Even when errors occur, unlike tangled human relationships, there is always a clear cause. And what you build actually goes on to solve someone's problems.

Every day, you can accumulate small moments of having your intent reach through. The feeling I could never find again in music was right here.

## Cor. Means "Heart" in Latin

The six months after leaving Recruit were truly painful. The borrowed money was gone in no time, and I had to part with my cherished instruments. Going to networking events only resulted in collecting business cards.

If that was the case, I decided I would be the one speaking.

In December 2023, I gave my first lightning talk at an Engineer Cafe LT event. My voice cracked, and my hands trembled. That same month, I founded Cor.Inc. Cor. means "heart" in Latin.

For the next year, I continued giving lightning talks somewhere every single week. Having deadlines meant building something every week. And then speaking about what I built in front of people. In March 2025, I spoke before an audience of about 2,000 people, including online attendees, at an AI-driven development study group in Tokyo. From that point on, inquiries about work began coming in.

## Verifying "Is That Really So?"

AI expanded my possibilities. It enabled me to build everything from end to end on my own.

However, if you move forward taking AI's answers as a given, you can end up running in circles over the same ground. That is why today, I pause and verify. Reading the primary sources. Measuring things myself.

Grift is an in-house product born from this mindset. Resumes can be embellished. Evidence cannot. That is why we do not turn candidates into scores; we output only verifiable facts. The decision-making is left to human judgment.

Our approach to local LLMs, which prevent confidential data from leaving the organization, comes from the exact same place. It exists because there are teams at a standstill, unsure whether it is safe to hand their confidential data over to external AI.

## Starting First by Listening

I know the pain of words failing to reach others.

That is why Cor. never jumps straight into building. First, we listen to find out where things are stalled. If necessary, we will say, "Don't build it yet."

I want to accumulate those small moments of "Ah, it came through"—both in work and in everyday life.

That is why a former trumpet player like me runs an AI company.
